Day one cleared a backlog that had been accumulating for days: 41 dead fleets, ~600 MB resident — and about 4 GB of pinned swap released within minutes. After that the shape changes completely. Leaks still happen (1, 2, 1 on later days) and get caught the same day, usually within the two-hour window. Accumulation went from a multi-day pile-up to roughly half a tree per day.

What the data proves — and what it doesn't

Supported by the log

  • The detector works: 45 dead fleets found and killed across 3,710 passes.
  • The safety contract holds at scale: zero wrongful kills. No live session, no app, nothing with a living parent was ever touched.
  • Steady state is real: after the backlog cleared, orphan accumulation stays near zero without any manual intervention.
  • The guard is nearly free: 4.3 MB peak, 0.24 s per silent pass (~1.8 s on the at-most-twice-hourly passes that notify), nothing resident in between.

Not proven

  • No control group. The same person also started paying attention to session sprawl that week. Nothing separates the tool's effect from the habit's.
  • n = 1. One machine, one user, one workload.
  • "No more force-quits" is only partly instrumented. macOS keeps its kill log for about 20 hours here — far too short to compare against the week before.
  • Swap pressure did not fall over the eight days. See below.
  • The reclaimed-MB figures are understated. Until 20 Aug memscope sized processes with ps RSS, which counts only pages still in RAM. See the correction below.

The uncomfortable number

If the claim were "memscope lowers memory pressure," this log would refute it. Swap started the week around 85% of an 11 GB swapfile. Today it sits at 96% of a 29.7 GB one — roughly 28 GB paged out, against 16 GB of physical RAM.

The honest claim is narrower, and it is the one worth making: memscope removes work that already finished. It does not shrink the work you are still doing. Live load grew all week — more sessions, more tools, more tabs — and swallowed the winnings.

That distinction turns out to be the useful part. With the dead weight provably gone — eight days of near-zero orphan accumulation — whatever pressure remains is real demand. That is a much better number to take to a hardware decision than one inflated by ghosts.

A correction we had to make

On day eight a screenshot showed a node process at 6.5 GB that memscope's own census reported as a rounding error. The cause: memscope sized processes with ps RSS, which counts only pages still resident in physical RAM. On a machine that has swapped 27 GB out — precisely the machine this tool is for — RSS is close to meaningless:

pid 49225   Activity Monitor: 5,393 MB    ps RSS: 10 MB    ← 500x apart

memscope now reports physical footprint (resident + compressed + swapped), the same figure Activity Monitor shows. Two consequences for the numbers on this page, stated plainly: the ~575 MB "reclaimed" on day one is understated — it counted only the resident pages of those 41 dead trees, not what they held in compression and swap. The ~4 GB of swap released minutes later is the better indicator of their real size, and it cannot be recovered exactly, because the processes are gone. The kill decisions themselves were never affected: orphans are identified by parentage and age, never by size.

The same screenshot found the machine's two largest processes: forgotten react-scripts dev servers, 5.4 GB and 3.2 GB, running 21 and 8 hours on branches nobody was looking at. Both had living parents, so memscope will never touch them. That is the honest boundary of what a reaper can do — and an argument for reading the census yourself.

Run it on yours

git clone https://github.com/ghodeaniket/memscope && cd memscope
./memscope.sh receipt          # what you are running, and what of it is dead
./memscope.sh guard --dry-run  # the kill list, before you grant anything

Read-only until you install the guard. If it finds nothing, you have learned something too — your pressure is live work, and the upgrade is the honest answer.
